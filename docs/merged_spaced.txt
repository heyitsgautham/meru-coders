PREFACE
Invested with a rhythm of higher spheres
The word was used as a hieratic means
For the release of the imprisioned spirit
Into communion with its comrade gods.
Sri Aurobindo
Since time immemorial the Rishis and poets of the land once known
as Bhāratavarṣa, and today as India, have consciously sought how
language could be moulded and words, sounds and rhythms used to
express the higher truths. It is this search that has led Sanskrit to its
sculpted perfection.
One of the important and highly developed tools of perfection
is chandas or metre. The word Chanda, derived from the root chad,
literally meaning that which delights. And in Sanskrit there is no
poetry without a chandas.
To understand and appreciate any poetical composition, a
knowledge and a feel of chandas is essential. Says the poet Dandi,
in his Kāvyādarśa, "the lore of chandas is the boat for those who
desire to cross the deep ocean of poetry."
सा विद्या नौस्तितीर्षूणां गम्भीरं काव्यसागरम् ।
sā vidyā naustitīrṣuṇāṁ gambhiraṁ kāvyasāgaram
To express any experience, feeling or emotion, the appropriate
chandas has to be chosen with great care, because each metre has
its own mood and movement. While using a particular metre, says
Kshemendra in his Suvṛttatilaka, "One has to see the rasa, the
mood, the nature of description and the context."
काव्ये रसानुसारेण वर्णनानुगुणेन च ।
कुर्वीत सर्ववृत्तानां विनियोगं विभागवित् ॥
kāvye rasānusāreṇa varṇanānugunena ca
kurvīta sarvavṛttānāṁ viniyogaṁ vibhāgavit

ii
In Sanskrit and in all Indian languages, the chanda is determined
by the arrangement of short and long syllables. The large number
of possible combinations has given rise to a large variety of chandas.
Sometimes the name of the chanda gives the clue to its
movement. For example, mandäkräntä means 'slow moving'. Most
of its syllables are long and heavy and it is ideal for expressing
pathos. Kalidasa has used it beautifully in Meghadütam where the
lovelorn Yaksa asks the cloud to be a messenger to his beloved.
श्यामास्वाङ्गं चकितहरिणीप्रेक्षणे दृष्टिपातं
वक्त्रच्छायां शशिनि शिखिनां बर्हभारेषु केशान् ।
उत्पश्यामि प्रतनुषु नदीवीचिषु भ्रूविलासान्
हन्तैकस्मिन् क्वचिदपि न ते चण्डि सादृश्यमस्ति ।।
śyāmāsvāngaṁ cakitahariṇīprekṣaṇe dṛṣṭipātaṁ
vaktrachāyāṁ śaśini śikhināṁ barhabhāreṣu keśān
utpaśyāmi pratanuṣu nadīvīciṣu bhrūvilāsān
hantaikasmin kvacidapi na te caṇdi sādṛśyamasti
I fancy to see your body in the priyangu creepers, your glances
in the eyes of the bewildered female deer, the beauty of your
face in the moon, your hair in the plumages of the peacocks,
and the sportive movements of your eyebrows in the rivers. But,
O misfortune! Nowhere in a single thing. O passionate one,
does your likeness exist.
Similarly, tvaritagati means 'fast gait' and contains many short
syllables. Here is a description of a gopi's meeting with Krishna. The
verse is taken from Gangadasa's chandomañjarī.
त्वरितगतिर्व्रजयुवतिस्तरणिसुता विपिनगता ।
मुररिपुणा रतिगुरुणा सहमिलिता प्रमदमिता ।।
tvaritagatirvrajayuvatistaraṇisutā vipinagatā
muraripuṇā ratiguruṇā sahamilitā pramadamitā
The maiden of vrajapura, while wandering in the forest near
the fast-flowing river Yamuna, became happy meeting Krishna,
the master of the art of love.

Drutavilambita means 'fast and slow'. This metre begins
with short syllables at a fast pace and later adopts a slow and tardy
rhythm using long syllables. Bhartrihari has used this metre in the
following verse of his Nītiśatakam.
विपदि धैर्यमथाभ्युदये क्षमा
सदसि वाक्पटुता युधि विक्रमः ।
यशसि चाभिरुचिर्व्यसनं श्रुतौ
प्रकृतिसिद्धमिदं हि महात्मनाम् ।।
vipadi dhairyamathābhyudaye kṣamā
sadasi vākpaṭutā yudhi vikramaḥ
yaśasi cābhirucirvyasanaṁ śrutau
prakṛtisiddhamidaṁ hi mahātmanām
Eloquence in the assembly; in the field
The puissant arm, the lion's heart; proud looks
Unshaken in defeat; but modest-kind
Mercy when victory crowns; passionate for books
High love of learning; thoughts to fame inclined;-
These things are natural to the noble mind.
At times the names of the chandas are derived from Nature.
For example, 'bhujangaprayāta' is named after the serpent and its
rhythm has a zigzag movement. Shankaracharya has written his
Bhavānyaṣṭakam in this metre.
न तातो न माता न बन्धुर्न दाता
न पुत्रो न पुत्री न भृत्यो न भर्ता ।
न जाया न विद्या न वृत्तिर्ममैव
गतिस्त्वं गतिस्त्वं त्वमेका भवानि ॥
na tāto na mātā na bandhurna dātā
na putro na putrī na bhṛtyo na bhartā |
na jāyā na vidyā na vṛttirmamaiva
gatistvaṁ gatistvaṁ tvamekā bhavāni ||
Neither father, nor mother, nor friend, nor giver, nor son, nor
daughter, nor servant, nor master, nor wife, nor learning, nor
occupation have I. You alone, O Bhavani, are my refuge.

re re cātaka sāvadhānamanasā mitra kṣaṇaṁ śrūyatām

O rainlark, rainlark, flitting near the cloud,
Attentive hear, winged friend, a friendly word.
All vapours are not like, the heavens that shround
Darkening; some drench the earth for noble fruit,
Some are vain thunderers wandering by with bruit:
Sue not to each thou seest then, O bird;
If humbly entreat thou must, let few have heard.
Though these are some interesting insights into Sanskrit metres,
there is no hard and fast rule. The same chanda is often used to
depict different moods, and different chandas can express the same
mood. It all depends on the inspiration and the mastery of the poet.
Chandovallarī is an attempt to introduce and explain the
structure of the principal chandas of Sanskrit prosody. More
importantly it attempts to give a direct feel and a living experience
of the various chandas.

INTRODUCTION
Almost all padyakāvyas or poetical compositions in Sanskrit
follow a metrical pattern. Therefore to understand and appreciate
them a knowledge of metrics or chandas is essential. The current
work aims to serve as an introduction to the main Sanskrit metres.
The examples given and the several indexes provided will enable
interested readers to become familiar with the commonly used
metres and identify them.
There are three main sections. Section one explains the place
of chandas in Indian literature, the history of chandas and
chandaśāstras, the classification of chandas and the rules governing
them. Section two deals with nineteen major and well-known metres
along with explanations and examples collected from various sources.
Each śloka is followed by an English translation. This section begins
with a verse of Valmiki, believed to be the first poet or ādikavi, as
an example of anuṣṭup metre, and ends with a verse of Kalidasa,
one of the greatest poets of Sanskrit literature, as an example of
sragdharā.
In section three there are several appendixes. The first appen-
dix gives the list of 26 genera or classes of chandas. The second
appendix contains the alphabetical list of gaṇas or syllabic feet.
The third appendix is an alphabetical list of the names of 121
chandas. The fourth appendix presents the explanation of all the
121 chandas along with their definitions and scheme of gaṇas. The
second, third and the fourth appendixes are all interlinked. The fifth
appendix has a note on mätrā chandas or metres regulated by syllabic
instants. Apart from these five appendixes this section also has
indexes to the names of the chandas and thier meanings, examples
to illustrate all varieties of upajāti metre, all the sources from which
examples appearing in the book are taken, all the ślokas appearing
in this book and a list of books referred to in this presentation.
Sampadananda Mishra

SIGNIFICANCE OF CHANDAS nd
IN THE LIGHT OF SRI AUROBINDO
Acharya Durga writes in his commentary on Nirukta (7.2)
of Yaskacharya: "No speech takes place without chanda",
(näcchandasi väguccarati). The same point is further elaborated
by Acharya Bharata who says in his Natyaśāstra (14.15): "No
speech is possible without chanda and a chanda without speech is
also equally impossible", (chandohino na śabdo'sti na chandaḥ
sabdavarjitam). Thus, one gathers from this that chanda is as
important as speech itself. But what exactly is a chanda? Rgveda
(1.164.24) says that all the seven chandas are measured by syllables,
(aksreṇa mimate saptavāṇīḥ). Supported by this Katyayana
defines chanda in his Ṛksarvānukramaṇī as "measurement of
syllables", (yad-akṣara-parimāṇaṁ tat chandaḥ). The following
words of Sri Aurobindo's add more clarity to the above statement
of Katyayana. He says that a chanda or metre means "a fixed and
balanced system of the measures of sound, mātrā ..." It is the
"ordered measures of the poetic spirit" and is the "right physical
basis for the poetic movement."3
A metre is generally regulated by several elements such as,
the number of metrical lines, number of syllables and their
arrangement or the number of syllabic instants, the arrangement of
pauses etc. In the case of Sanskrit chandas or metres it is seen that
the main principle governing the metres of the Vedas is measurement
by number of syllables. This was also extended to the classical
Sanskrit metres. However, they differ in many cases from those of
Vedic metres where it is the number of syllables that matters. But
in classical Sanskrit metres, the arrangement of syllables by short
and long, plays an important role. In Vedic Sanskrit it is not necessary
that all gayatri chandas should have the same arrangement of
syllables, but the number of syllables should be same in all quarters.
vii
In classical Sanskrit the metres regulated by number of
syllables (aksaravṛttas) are divided into three categories such as,
samavṛttass or metres having same number of syllables in each
line with same arrangement; ardhasamavṛttas or metres where
each alternate line has same arrangement of sounds; visamavṛttas
or metres having uneven stanzas with different arrangements. Based
on arrangement of syllables, one particular metre differs from
another. Thus, we have a large variety of metres. In case of the
above metres if any quarter of a metre has one or two syllables less
or more than the prescribed number then the metre is considered to
be defective. This is called chandobhanga (or breaking the rules of
the metre) which is not permitted by prosodians. But in the case of
Vedic metres the absence or excess of one or two syllables in a
quarter of any chanda is neither considered to be a defect nor by
that does a chanda change into another. So, the Aitareyabrāhmaṇa
(1.6) says, na vā ekākṣreṇa chandāmsi viyanti na dvābhyām,
and Kauṣitakībrāhmaṇa (27.1) adds, na hyekākṣareṇa
anyacchando bhavati na dvābhyām. But to recognise the places
where more or less syllables occur the Vedic prosodians used some
terms before the name of the chanda. For example, in a gayatri
metre, if one syllable is less in a quarter then it is called nicṛd gayatri,
if two syllables are less then it is virāț gayatrī; if one syllable is
more it is called bhurik gayatri, if two syllables are more then it is
svarāt gayatrī. So is the case with other metres as well. The
Vedic prosodians have also prescribed how to fill the metre by means
of vyuha (which ordinarily implies breaking sandhi in case of ya
and va). For example, the first line of the famous gayatrimantra
(tat savitur vareṇyam) has one syllable less. So to fill it, one can
break ya in varenyaminto i and a or i and ya and instead of
vareṇyam, one can chant vareṇi-am or vareṇi-yam. These are
some cases where Vedic metres are more flexible than those of
classical ones.
Apart from the akşaravṛttas, the classical Sanskrit has also
another metre called jātivṛtta or mätrāvṛtta which is absent in
Vedic literature. Here, the metre is regulated not by the number of
viii
syllables but by the syllabic instants or mātrās. āryā and vaitālīya
are considered to be the two main varieties of mātrāvṛttas.
This, in short, is a brief outline of the Sanskrit metres both
Vedic and classical.
Now with regard to metres, several questions may come to
mind such as: what do we demand from the metres? Are metres
artificial or natural? Is there any greater significance of the metres
than their physical compositions? What do different arrangements
stand for? etc. In ancient texts on the chandas, we do not find
much helpful answer to these questions. But Sri Aurobindo's views
on the subject provide some deep insights into the understanding of
real significance of the chandas.
Chandas: The most Natural Mould of Expression
Chanda or metre is an essential part of poetry. It is this part
that makes poetry differ from prose. In prose intonation is flat. But
poetic intonation, as Sri Aurobindo observes, has "with it a heightened
or gathered intensity of language, a deepened vibrating intensity of
rhythm, an intense inspiration in the thought substance."4 With these
elements poetry is capable of lifting us "to a higher scale of
consciousness which expresses things common with uncommon
power of vision and of utterance." Chanda is a "spontaneous form"
of this uncommon power.
Sri Aurobindo observes that there is "a greater natural power"
and "intenser value of sound" inherent within the metrical forms.
He says: "Metre, on the contrary, is the most natural mould of
expression for certain states of creative emotion and vision, it is
much more natural and spontaneous than a non-metrical form, the
emotion expresses itself best and most powerfully in a balanced
rather than in a loose and shapeless rhythm." This is the reason
why ancient Indians expressed everything including science,
philosophy, law etc., in metrical forms.
Metrical forms, of course, help the memory because they
have an easy durability inherent in them. But this is not the only
reason why ancient Indians were fond of metrical forms. They were
ix
able to memorise aslo huge passages of prose from Brāhmaṇa texts
as accurately as the metrical hymns in the Vedas and Upanishads.
So for them, metre was something deeper than its physical form.
Sri Aurobindo makes it very clear when he says: "The tradition of
metre is not a vain and foolish convention followed by the great
poets of the past in primitive ignorance unconscious of their own
bondage, it is in spite of its appearance of human convention a law
of Nature, an innermost mind-nature, a highest speech-nature.
The Chandas and Creation
All creations have a base to proceed on. This base is constituted
with oneness and sameness and a superstructure of diversity. There
is the possibility of a highest creation only when there is an intense
power of basic unity and sameness constituting the supporting base
for the "intensest power of appropriate and governed diversity".
Similarly in poetic speech, a metre gives this intense power of basic
unity and sameness, and the rhythmic vibration in a metre gives the
"intensest power of expressive diversity." The ancient Rishis were
aware of these facts, and to them metre was not a lifeless formal
construction but a fact of reproduction of great creative world-
rhythms.
Bhartrihari, a grammarian-philosopher of 6th century A.D.,
says in his Vākyapadiya (1.121) that "this universe came forth
from the chandas only", chandobhya eva prathamametad viśvam
vyavartata. This statement of Bhartrihari has an echo in
Sri Aurobindo who was convinced that there is a subtle truth in the
"fixed and recurring measures of sound". He believed like the Vedic
Rishis that "the spirit of creation framed all movements of the world
by chandas, in certain fixed rhythms of the formative word, and it is
because they are faithful to cosmic metres that the basic world
movements unchangingly endure. A balanced harmony maintained
by a system of subtle recurrences is the foundation of immortality
in created things, and metrical movement is simply creative sound
grown conscious of this secret of its own powers."

A True Picture of Chandas
Sri Aurobindo draws "a parallel between the elements of Man
and the elements of Metre" when he gives a true picture of a metre.
He says that in man there is the outward food plasm and within it
there is the vital or sensational man. Within the vital man there is
the emotional or impressional man and further within that there is
the intellectual man who governs the others. Again, within him there
is the delight of the Spirit and within this delight there sits the Spirit
itself, the Lord of all these. In metre also similar elements exist.
Sri Aurobindo explains that the quantitative or accentual
arrangement forms the body of a metre. Within this body there lies
the sensational element which deals with the arrangement of pauses
and sounds, such as assonance, alliteration, composition of related
and varying letters etc. Within this there is the pure emotional
movement of the verse. Within these three elements (the body, the
sensational element and the emotional movement of the verse), there
is the intellectual element which brings the element of restraint,
management and subordination to the law of harmony. This element
is the driver of the chariot of sound. Without this the harmony is not
possible in a verse. Again, there is the poetic delight and within this
delight there is the spirit. This spirit is an unanalysable reality behind
metre, style and diction, and this is the element which distinguishes
one poet from another. The august poetic delight is the outflow of
this "mighty spirit, its sense of life and power and its pleasure in the
use of that power with no ulterior motive beyond its own delight."8
Significance of Different Arrangements of Sounds in Chandas
Among all important elements of a metre different
arrangements of sounds occupy a significant place. Sri Aurobindo
observes that different arrangements of sounds have different
spiritual values. He says that these arrangements "tend to produce
by virtue of the fixed succession of sounds a fixed spiritual
atmosphere and a given type of emotional exaltation, and the mere
creative power of sounds though a metrical thing is yet mean to
spirit, is very great on the material but ascending in force through
the moral and intellectual, it culminates on the emotional plane. It is
a factor of first importance in music and poetry."
Therefore, Sri Aurobindo says that when we speak of a
particular metre "we are speaking of a certain spiritual force working
through emotion into the material shape of a fixed mould of
rhythmical sound which obeys both in its limiting sameness and in
its variations the law of the spirit within.'
Chandas: the gate of inspiration
9910
According to Sri Aurobindo, for a poet, "management of metre
is the gate of his inspiration and law of his success." As long as a
poet does not have a right metrical mood, the ideas and feelings
"will not run into the right form, the words will not take their right
places, the syllables will not fall into a natural harmony." It is only
when a poet finds a right metre suitable to the spirit and emotion of
the poetry he is creating, that everything fits in place. In Sri
Aurobindo's words: “Ideas and words come rapidly and almost as
rapidly take their places, as in a well-ordered assembly where
everyone knows his seat."¹¹ There should not be any sort of effort
to create the metre and it should be a spontaneous movement. Sri
Aurobindo says that "when the metre has to be created with effort,
everything else has to be done with effort, and the result has to be
worked on over and over again before it satisfies."12
Conclusion
The above views of Sri Aurobindo not only provide new
guidelines to the study of chandas, but also reveal the secret truth
of chandas and throw a new light, a new vision on the world of
poets and poetry.

SECTION I
1.1. Introduction
The vast body of Sanskrit literature, which we have with us
today, is the legacy of Rishis both ancient and modern. It is an
inexhaustible wealth of inspiration for the student of poetic metres.
The study of metrics has a long tradition as an important branch
of Vedic learning. The text that deals with the rules of metrics is
called chandaśāstra and is one of the six Vedāngas or limbs of the
Vedas.¹
The chanda itself is considered by the eminent grammarian,
Panini, as the two legs of the Vedas.2
1.1.1. Chandaśāstra and texts on chanda
The chandaśāstra is said to be the first systematic treatise on
chandas. It was written in about 150 B.C. by Pingalacharya.³
According to the 'History of Indian Literature', the
Sankhyāyana-śrautasūtra contains several chapters on prosody.
Other Sanskrit texts dealing with prosody or metrics are Ṛgveda-

1.2. Rules of Chandas
This section deals with the technical terms in Chandaśāstra
and the essential rules relating to chandas.
1.2.1. Chanda or Metre
Literary compositions (kāvyas) in Sanskrit may be in the form
of prose (gadya) or in the form of verse (padya). A poetical stanza
or verse in Sanskrit is called padya. Generally a padya or verse
contains four pādas or quarters or metrical lines.
Sanskrit verses are classified into groups and sub-groups
according to-
(a) the number of syllables or syllabic instants they contain
in each quarter and
(b) the position or placement of short or long syllables within
the verse.
Such groups and sub-groups are called chandas.
1.2.2. Padya or Verse
As mentioned above any metrical composition in Sanskrit is
called a padya or verse.
1.2.3. Pada or Quarter
All verses in Sanskrit contain four lines. Each line is called a
pāda or quarter. The first two pādas form the first half of a verse
and the remaining two pādas form the last half of the verse.
A pāda or quarter is regulated either by number of syllables
(akṣara) or by the number of syllabic instants (mātrā).
1.2.3.1. Akşara or Syllable
Aksara is as much a word as can be pronounced distinctly at
one go or by one effort of the voice. So a vowel with or without
one or more consonants is considered as one syllable.
A syllable can be short (laghu) or long (guru), depending on
whether its vowel is short or long.

1.2.3.1.a. Laghu or Short Syllables
5
The vowels अ (a), इ (i), उ (u), ऋ (r) and ल (l) are short.
Whenever any of these is used in a verse separately or with one or
more consonants, it will be considered as a short syllable. For
example, (ka), fo (ki), etc. are short syllables.
The crescent sign (~) is used to represent a short syllable in
scansion and metrical analysis.
1.2.3.1.b. Guru or Long Syllables
The vowels आ (ā), ई (l), ऊ (ū), ॠ (r), ए (e), ऐ (ai), ओ (o), औ
(au) are long. Whenever any of these is used in a verse separately
or with one or more consonants, it will be considered as long. For
example, (kā), (ki), etc. are long syllables.
The horizontal bar (-) is used to represent a long syllable in
scansion and metrical analysis.
However, a short vowel becomes long under three conditions
given below.6
(a). If a vowel is followed by an anusvāra. For example, कं (kam), i (gam), etc.
(b) If a vowel is followed by a visarga. For example, कः (kah), गः (gah), etc.
(c) If a vowel is followed by a conjunct consonant. For example, गन्ध (gandha). Here, even though ga is short, it has to be considered as long because it is followed by the conjunct consonant न्य (ndha). While counting the syllables, ग (ga) and (n) should be counted together as गन् (gan) and घ (dha) as a separate syllable.
At the end of a quarter a short syllable can be considered as
long and a long syllable short as per the requirement of the chanda.

1.2.3.2. Mātrā or Metrical Unit
A unit of metrical quantity is called a mātrā, or 'instant'. A
mātrā or 'instant' denotes the time required to utter a short vowel.
All short vowels are regarded as consisting of one mātrā. All long
vowels and dipthongs are regarded as consisting of two mātrās.
For example, the word तप (tapa) contains two (1+1) mātrās because there are two short vowels त् + अ, प् + अ (t+a, p+a). The word ताप (tāpa) contains three (2+1) mātrās because there is one long vowel and one short vowel त् + आ, प् + अ (t+ā, p+a)
1.2.4. Verse Classification
All padyas or verses in Sanskrit are classified as either vṛtta
or jāti.
1.2.4.1. Vṛttattachanda
A vṛttattachanda is that which is regulated by the number
and positions of syllable in each pada or quarter.
Vṛttattachanda is further sub-divided into three categories,
such as:
(a) samavṛtta or even metres
(b) ardhasamavṛtta or half-even metres
(c) visamavṛtta or uneven metres
1.2.4.1.a. Samavṛtta or Even Metres
Verses in which the quarters contain an equal number of
syllables are called samavṛtta. anuṣṭup, indravajrā etc. are
examples of this type.

The prosodians divide verses of this type into twenty-six
genera, according to the number of syllables in each of the four
pādas or quarters. This number varies from one to twenty-six. Each

for metres with eight syllables is anuṣṭup and for metres with eleven

1.2.5. Yati or Metrical Pause
The metrical pause in Sanskrit is called yati or virama. This
metrical pause is of two types, the sentential pause and the harmonic
pause.
1.2.5.1. Sentential Pause
The sentential pause occurs at the end of the hemistich i.e. at the
middle of a verse and again at the end. The pause at the end of the
hemistich is marked by a single vertical line (|) called half pause and
at the end of the verse by two vertical lines (||) called full pause.
1.2.5.2. Harmonic Pause
The harmonic pause, technically called caesura, occurs after
some particular syllables within the quarter of a verse. It varies
from metre to metre. For example in sikhariṇī metre the harmonic
pause occurs after 6th and 17th syllables of each quarter. In
śārdulavikrīḍitam it occurs after 12th and 19th syllables of each
quarter.
1.2.6. Scanning of the Chandas
When a verse is scanned, it is split into constituent syllables, long or
short. However, for the purpose of classification and concise
definition of the chandas, the Sanskrit prosodians use groups of
syllables called gaṇas.
1.2.6.1. Gaṇa
In metres regulated by syllables, each metrical line or quarter of a
verse is divided into groups of three syllables each. Each group is
distinguished from the others by its own particular arrangement of
syllables. These groups are called gaṇas or syllabic feet.

(f) Where all the first syllable is long and the other two short
it is 'bha' gana.
(g) Where the middle syllable is long and the other two short
it is 'ja' gana.
(h) Where the last syllable is long and the other two short it
is 'sa' gaṇa.
Apart from these eight gaṇas there are other two gaṇas named
ga and la. ga represents a single long syllable and la represents a
single short syllable.

MATRA CHANDAS OR METRES OF SYLLABIC INSTANTS
In the earlier sections we have dealt with samavrittas or metres having
same number of syllables and same arrangements of sound in all the
four lines. In this additional section a note on the metres regulated by
the syllabic instants or mātrās is given.
Among the metres regulated by syllabic instants āryā, vaitālīya and
mātrāsamaka are the three major divisions. The most common variety
of matravrittas is ārya. pathyā, vipulā, capalā, mukhacapalā,
jaghanacapalā, gīti, upagīti, udgīti and äryägīti are the nine sub-varieties
of aryā. The last four in the list are more popular than the earlier five.
So, the definitions of arya and its four popular divisions are given here
with examples.

āryā
In this variety the first and third quarters each contain twelve mātrās
and the second eighteen mātrās and the fourth fifteen (12-18-12-15).
The Āryāsaptasati of Govardhana is entirely composed in this metre.
Example:
जयति स नगाधिराजो निकेतने यस्य निर्जरस्तुत्या ।
जगतः शुभाय जाता माता मातङ्गवदनस्य ॥
jayati sa nagādhirājo niketane yasya nirjarastutyā
jagataḥ śubhāya jātā mātā mätangavadanasya
Devistotramañjarī of Vasishtha Ganapati Muni, 1

gīti
When the first and third quarters of a verse contain twelve mātrās
each and the second and fourth have eighteen each (12-18-12-18)
then the metre is called gīti.
Example:
श्रियमातनोतु सुकृतं पुष्णातु ततान्यघानि मुष्णातु ।
दिशतु च भूयो भूयस्तव भगवानष्टमूर्तिरिष्टानि ॥

Chandovallarī
In the vaitālīya group the various metres are aupachandasika,
āpātalikā, caruhāsinī and aparāntikā. Of these vaitālīya and
aupachandasika are more popular. So, the definitions of these two
with examples are given here:
vaitālīya
In vaitālīya, the first and the third lines contain fourteen mātrās in
which after six mātrās the arrangement of syllables by short and long
is: long-short-long-short-long. The second and fourth llines of this
metre have sixteen syllabic instants in which after eight mātrās the
arrangement of syllable by short and long is: long-short-long-short-
long. The first six mātrās in the first and third lines and the first eight
mātrās in the second and fourth lines can be a mixture of short or
long syllables.
Example:
उपकारपरः स्वभावतः सततं सर्वजनस्य सज्जनः ।
असतामनिशं तथाप्यहो गुरुहृद्रोगकारी तदुन्नतिः ।।
upakāraparaḥ svabhāvataḥ satataṁ sarvajanasya sajjanaḥ
asatāmaniśaṁ tathāpyaho guruhṛdrogakārī tadunnatiḥ
Śiśupālavadham of Magha, 16.22
aupachandasika
This chanda is exactly like that of vaitālīya but each line here has one
long syllable extra at the end. So, here the first and third lines have 16
mātrās and, second and fourth lines have 18 mātrās.
Example:
जगदेतदनारतं पचन्तं महतोऽस्मादपि पुष्करान्महान्तम्
तपसा निरुपाधिकेन गम्यं परमात्मानमजं शिवाख्यमीडे ।।
jagadeteadanärataṁ pacantaṁ mahato'smādapi puṣkarānmahāntam
tapasā nirupādhikena gamyaṁ paramātmānamajaṁ śivākhyamīḍe
Śivaśatakam of Vasishtha Ganapati Muni, 51

these mātrāsamaka and pādākulaka being popular, we provide the
definitions of these two with examples.
mātrāsamaka & pādākulaka
The metre called mātrāsamaka consists of four quarters each of which
contains sixteen syllabic instants. The most general variety of this
metre is that in which the ninth syllabic instant is short and the last is
long. The different varities of this metre arise from particular syllabic
instants being short or long. A mixed variety of several varities of this
metre gives rise to pādākulaka. In pādākulaka there is no other
restriction than that each quarter should have sixteen syllabic instants.
Example of mātrāsamaka
छिन्नां भिन्नां सुतरां सन्नामन्नाभावादभितः खिन्नाम् ।
एतां पातुं भरतक्षोणीं जाये जिष्णोः कुरु मां शक्तम् ॥
chinnāaṁ bhinnāṁ sutarāṁ sannāmannābhāvādabhitaḥ khinnām
etām pātuṁ bharatakṣonīm jāye jisnoḥ kuru māṁ śaktam
Indrāṇīsaptaśati of Vasishtha Ganapati Muni, 399
